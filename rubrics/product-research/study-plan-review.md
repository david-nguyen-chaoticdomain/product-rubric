## Purpose

Use this rubric to review a research plan before the study runs. Most research waste is created at this stage and discovered afterward: questions no method in the plan can answer, the wrong participants recruited carefully, sessions that walk participants past the evidence the team needed. A plan review is the cheapest quality control in research, because every flaw it catches costs a sentence to fix now and a study to fix later.

The review judges the plan as a design for producing evidence. It does not judge the product being researched, the researcher's skill, or whether the topic is worth studying in the abstract. It judges the fit between four things: what the team needs to learn, the questions the plan asks, the method chosen to answer them, and the people and materials the method will run on.

If the input contains no plan at all (a topic, a vague intention, a request to "do research"), say so, and return the Context requests below as the outline of the plan that needs writing. Do not invent a plan and review your own invention.

## Context requests

Before judging, check the plan for the context below. If an item is missing and this is a conversation, ask for it, in one grouped round, before issuing verdicts. If it cannot be supplied, judge the affected criteria as Missing and name the gap.

Ask for:

- The decision or decisions this study feeds: what the team will do differently depending on what it learns. (Needed for Answerable questions and for judging whether the plan's scope is right.)
- Who commissioned the study and what they already believe, when that is not in the plan. A plan built to confirm a belief needs different scrutiny than one built to explore. (Needed for Risks, bias, and ethics.)
- The timeline and recruiting constraints the plan was written under, when they are not stated: deadlines, available panels, incentive budget. A lean plan under a hard deadline can be Ready where the same plan with open resources would not be. (Needed for fairness in every criterion.)

## Criteria

Judge each criterion Ready, Needs work, or Missing, using the Scoring section. Ground every verdict in the plan's own text: quote the question, the screener line, or the session step you are judging.

### 1. Answerable questions
The research questions can actually be answered by evidence a study can produce.
- What to examine: Each research question for whether it is specific enough to recognize an answer when one appears; whether it asks about behavior and experience (answerable) or about predictions and preferences stated in the abstract (what people say they would do, which studies answer badly); whether the list is a real set of questions or a topic area wearing question marks.
- What good looks like: A short list of questions, each of which the team could mark answered or unanswered at the end of the study without argument.
- What bad looks like: "Understand the user journey" as a question; questions whose answers are already decided; a list so long that no single study could touch half of it.

### 2. Method fits the questions
The chosen method produces the kind of evidence the questions need.
- What to examine: The pairing of each major question to the method that will answer it: interviews for how and why, observation and usability sessions for what people do, surveys for how many and how often, diary methods for behavior over time. Whether the plan claims a method will deliver what it structurally cannot (a five-person interview study reporting percentages, an unmoderated test explaining motives).
- What good looks like: Every question has a named method that can answer it, and the claims the plan promises stay inside what the method produces.
- What bad looks like: One method asked to do everything; depth questions assigned to a survey; prevalence questions assigned to a handful of interviews.

### 3. Participants match the questions
The people recruited are the people the questions are about.
- What to examine: The participant definition against the research questions: whether the screening criteria select on the traits and behaviors the questions depend on (recent experience with the task, not just demographics); whether the plan recruits the people who struggle as well as the people who succeed; whether the sample size is argued from the questions rather than copied from habit; who is excluded, and whether the exclusion quietly removes the evidence.
- What good looks like: A screener a recruiter could run without interpretation, selecting for the experiences the questions are about, in numbers the plan justifies.
- What bad looks like: "Users, 25 to 44" as the whole definition for a study about a specialized workflow; recruiting only current happy customers to learn why people leave.

### 4. Scope and decisions
The plan states what it will not learn, and the findings have somewhere to go.
- What to examine: Whether the plan names its exclusions (topics, audiences, questions deliberately out of scope); whether each intended output connects to a decision, a design question, or an open team debate; whether the plan has a stopping sense, a point at which the team agrees it has learned enough to act.
- What good looks like: A reader can say what this study will not answer, and name the meeting or decision the findings feed.
- What bad looks like: Scope defined only by what is included, so every stakeholder question joins the study; findings promised to "inform the roadmap" with no decision, owner, or date attached.

### 5. Procedure and materials
The study as specified could actually be run, the same way, by the person holding the plan.
- What to examine: For session studies: the session flow, task wording, and timing, and whether tasks are specified at all or left to improvisation. For any study: the materials to prepare (prototypes at the right fidelity, stimuli, consent forms), a pilot or dry run step, and the capture plan (recording, notes, where the evidence will live). Whether task or question wording is written down, because unwritten wording gets invented live, differently each session.
- What good looks like: A competent colleague could run this study from the plan alone and produce comparable evidence.
- What bad looks like: "Conduct 6 to 8 interviews" as the entire procedure; tasks described by their intent ("see if they can check out") instead of their wording; no pilot, so the first real participant is the test of the materials.

### 6. Risks, bias, and ethics
The plan sees its own ways of going wrong, and treats participants properly.
- What to examine: Demand characteristics and confirmation paths (questions or tasks that telegraph the desired answer; stakeholders selecting friendly participants); consent, recording permission, and data handling; incentive appropriateness; sensitive topics and the plan for participant distress or withdrawal; whether the plan protects the evidence from the team's hopes (a plan for hearing what nobody wanted to hear).
- What good looks like: The plan names its most likely bias and says what is being done about it; consent and data handling are specified, not assumed.
- What bad looks like: Recruiting arranged so the team only meets people predisposed to praise; recording planned with consent as an afterthought; no word anywhere about what happens to the data.

## Scoring

This rubric gives no numeric score. Each criterion receives one verdict:

- **Ready**: the plan handles this criterion well enough to field. Minor polish may be suggested, but nothing here puts the evidence at risk.
- **Needs work**: the criterion is addressed, but a specific flaw in it will weaken, skew, or waste the evidence unless it is fixed first. The finding names the flaw and the fix.
- **Missing**: the plan does not address this criterion at all, or addresses it so vaguely that it cannot be run or judged. The finding names what needs to be written.

Overall readiness, stated once at the top of the report, in exactly one of these forms:

- **Ready to field**: no criterion below Ready except, at most, one Needs work whose fix is small and named.
- **Field after fixes**: one or more Needs work verdicts, none of them Missing, with the fixes listed in the closing section.
- **Not ready**: any criterion is Missing, or the method and the questions do not fit, because fielding this plan would spend participants and time producing evidence that cannot answer the questions.

A plan can be short and Ready. Verdicts judge fitness for the study's own stated purpose and constraints, not the plan's length, polish, or resemblance to a template.

## Output format

Return the review in this structure, in Markdown:

1. **Overall readiness: [Ready to field / Field after fixes / Not ready]**, followed by one sentence of the reason in plain terms.
2. A short paragraph naming the plan's strongest point and the single risk most likely to damage the evidence if the study ran as written.
3. One section per criterion, in the order above, showing its verdict and its findings, each finding tied to the plan's quoted text or to the named absence. For a Needs work verdict, each finding includes the fix at the level of a sentence the planner can paste or act on.
4. A closing section, **Fixes before fielding**, listing the required changes in priority order: first the ones that protect the evidence, then the ones that protect the participants, then the ones that protect the team's time.

Example of a finding at the standard this rubric expects:
- Acceptable: "Needs work (Participants match the questions): the screener recruits 'people who shop online weekly,' but research question 2 asks why shoppers abandon a first purchase. People who shop weekly have, by definition, completed purchases; the screener should recruit people who started a first purchase in the last 90 days and did not finish it."
- Not acceptable: "The participant criteria could be more targeted." (It names no question, no mismatch, and no fix.)

Finally, end the report with the tracking summary block, in exactly this form. Every value in the block is copied or counted from the judgments in this report; the block adds no new judgment of its own. It is the record a team keeps when it runs this rubric repeatedly and wants to see whether its work is improving.

```
Rubric summary, for tracking
Rubric: Study Plan Review v1.0 · Date: {the date of this run} · Artifact: {the artifact's name as given in the request, or: not named}
Criteria: Ready {n} · Needs work {n} · Missing {n} · Not judged {n}
Gate criteria not met: {the criteria whose grade held the verdict below Ready to field, or: none}
Result: {the overall verdict, exactly as stated at the top of this report}
```

## Honesty rules

- Judge the plan that was submitted, against the decisions it says it feeds. Do not downgrade a plan for failing to answer questions it never set out to answer, and do not upgrade it for rigor that serves no stated decision.
- Distinguish a flaw from a taste. Plans differ legitimately in style and depth; a verdict below Ready must name the evidence that would be harmed, not merely the reviewer's preference for another format.
- Do not assume unstated good practice. If consent, screening logic, or task wording is not in the plan or the supplied context, it is Missing or part of a finding, not silently presumed to exist.
- A plan review cannot promise good findings. Even a Ready plan produces evidence whose quality depends on execution and on participants; say so in the report when the overall verdict is Ready to field, in one line.
- Where the plan's quality depends on context the review could not get (the decision the study feeds, the constraints it was written under), say which verdicts are provisional for that reason rather than presenting them as settled.
