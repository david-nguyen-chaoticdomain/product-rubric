## Purpose

Use this rubric to review a battlecard before it reaches the field. A battlecard is a peculiar document: it is read in seconds, mid-call, by someone whose credibility is on the line, and nearly everything in it is a claim about another company's product made by people who want to beat that company. Its failure modes are correspondingly specific. A claim that was true last year and is false now does not just fail to persuade; it teaches the buyer that the rep's other claims are also negotiable. A card that lists only the competitor's weaknesses sends a rep into a deal unprepared for the two things the competitor genuinely does better, which the buyer is about to say out loud.

This review follows the anatomy and disciplines published by Klue, the competitive enablement company whose battlecard guidance is a standard reference in product marketing: a card carries a competitor overview, questions to ask, landmines to lay, objection handling, pricing, and why we win and why we lose, kept short, linked to proof, and continuously updated, with the strongest content drawn from win and loss evidence rather than internal opinion.

The review judges the card. It does not adjudicate the rivalry, and it does not write competitive strategy. If the input is a competitive analysis rather than a field document, say so and stop; that is the Competitive Analysis Review's subject.

## Criteria

Judge each criterion Holds, Needs work, or Missing, using the Scoring section. Every judgment cites the card's own text.

### 1. Claims about the competitor are sourced and dated
Every material claim the card makes about the competitor can be traced to evidence a skeptic could check, with a date on it.
- What to examine: Feature and capability claims, pricing claims, and limitation claims, each with their source named (documentation, a dated announcement, win/loss interviews, a review site) or visibly unsourced. Pricing above all, since pricing pages change quietly and a rep quoting last year's price loses the room. Claims drawn from win and loss evidence, the buyer's own words, which Klue's guidance treats as the strongest material a card can carry, distinguished from claims drawn from internal opinion about the competitor.
- What good looks like: A reader can put a finger on any claim and know where it came from and as of when, and the claims a rep is likeliest to repeat aloud are the best-sourced claims on the card.
- What bad looks like: "Their reporting is weak," stated flatly, sourced nowhere, in a card last touched before the competitor shipped a reporting overhaul.

### 2. The competitor's strengths are stated honestly
The card describes the competitor the way a fair-minded buyer would, including where the competitor genuinely wins.
- What to examine: A strengths or "why they win" section with real content, not a token sentence before the attack. The competitor's actual positioning and best-fit customers described in terms their own users would accept. Whether the card's criticisms are the ones buyers themselves raise, or only the ones this team's product happens to answer. Strawman tells: a competitor described as expensive and feature-poor and hard to use and badly supported, all at once, which no surviving company is.
- What good looks like: A rep reading this card could predict the pitch the competitor's best rep would give, and respect it enough to prepare for it.
- What bad looks like: A competitor with no customers for any discernible reason; every strength reframed within the same sentence as a hidden weakness, which reps learn to distrust as quickly as buyers do.

### 3. The card arms both directions: wins, losses, and landmines
The rep gets why we win, why we lose, the questions that tilt the conversation, and the topics to steer away from or prepare for.
- What to examine: A why-we-win set tied to the competitor specifically, not the generic differentiators from the messaging guide. A why-we-lose set that names real losing patterns from deals, not none recorded. Landmines in Klue's sense: questions a rep can ask that surface needs the competitor serves badly, phrased as questions a buyer can be asked with a straight face. Traps acknowledged: the subjects the competitor's reps raise against this product, with a prepared answer for each, including the ones whose honest answer begins with a concession.
- What good looks like: A rep finishes the card knowing how this deal is won, how it is usually lost, and what they will say in the first ninety seconds after the competitor's name comes up.
- What bad looks like: Why-we-win present and enthusiastic; why-we-lose absent entirely, leaving every rep to discover the losing patterns privately, one deal at a time.

### 4. Usable in the moment: say it, show it
The card can be absorbed in seconds and used aloud: talk tracks a rep can actually speak, with proof attached to the claims they will lean on.
- What to examine: The say and show test. Say: objection responses and differentiators written as speakable language, the objection itself quoted the way buyers phrase it, rather than as internal analysis nobody could say on a call. Show: a proof asset behind the central claims (a micro case study, a measured result, a buyer quote) or an honest absence. Size discipline: the card fits its moment, readable at a glance mid-call, with depth linked rather than embedded; a buried quick dismiss for the early-stage case, where the competitor must be dispatched in one sentence so the conversation can move on.
- What good looks like: A rep on a live call finds the objection they are hearing, reads the response once, and says something close to it, with a proof point ready if the buyer pushes.
- What bad looks like: Three pages of competitor history and funding news above the fold, talk tracks written as bullet-pointed features, proof nowhere, and the one sentence a rep actually needs discoverable only by search.

### 5. Freshness is owned
The card carries its date, its owner, and the events that should trigger a refresh, and it is inside its own freshness window.
- What to examine: A visible updated date and a named owner or owning team. Refresh triggers named (competitor releases, pricing changes, a loss pattern emerging) rather than a vague promise to keep it current. Internal consistency with the date: claims phrased in a present tense the intervening months may have falsified. Where the card sits in a set, whether the set's cards are visibly maintained to the same standard or are a mix of current and fossil, which reads to a rep as all fossil.
- What good looks like: A rep can see at a glance when this card was last verified and trust it accordingly, and someone identifiable is embarrassed if it goes stale.
- What bad looks like: No date, no owner, and a competitor pricing table from before their publicly announced pricing change; the card is not out of date in one place, it is untrustworthy as an object.

## Scoring

This rubric gives no numeric score. Each criterion receives one verdict:

- **Holds**: the card meets this criterion; a rep can rely on it in this respect.
- **Needs work**: the criterion is attempted but a specific gap undermines it. The finding names the gap and what would repair it.
- **Missing**: the card makes no attempt at this criterion (no sources anywhere, no why-we-lose at all), or the attempt is so thin it cannot carry weight.

Overall verdict, stated once at the top, in exactly one of these forms:

- **Safe to put in a rep's hands**: no criterion below Holds except at most one Needs work.
- **Usable with the noted gaps**: any other combination short of the line below; the gaps travel with the card into live deals until they are repaired.
- **Do not circulate yet**: any criterion Missing on Claims about the competitor are sourced and dated, or The card arms both directions. In this state the card endangers the people it was meant to help, which is the one outcome worse than no card at all.

## Output format

Return the review in this structure, in Markdown:

1. **Verdict: [Safe to put in a rep's hands / Usable with the noted gaps / Do not circulate yet]**, with one sentence of reason, and a line stating what evidence, if any, was available to the review beyond the card itself.
2. One section per criterion, in the order above, with its verdict and findings tied to specific claims and sections of the card.
3. A closing section, **Before this goes to the field**, in priority order: unsourced or stale claims about the competitor first, then the missing losing-side material, then usability and freshness.

Example of a finding at the standard this rubric expects:
- Acceptable: "Missing: the pricing section quotes the competitor at $49 per seat with no source and no date. The competitor announced consumption pricing in March, which the card's own news link shows. Until the pricing claim is re-sourced and dated, a rep who repeats it will be corrected by the first buyer who has seen a current quote."
- Not acceptable: "The battlecard should be kept up to date." (It names no claim, no date, and no repair.)

Finally, end the report with the tracking summary block, in exactly this form. Every value in the block is copied or counted from the judgments in this report; the block adds no new judgment of its own. It is the record a team keeps when it runs this rubric repeatedly and wants to see whether its work is improving.

```
Rubric summary, for tracking
Rubric: Battlecard Review v1.0 · Date: {the date of this run} · Artifact: {the artifact's name as given in the request, or: not named}
Criteria: Holds {n} · Needs work {n} · Missing {n}
Gate criteria not met: {the criteria whose grade held the verdict below Safe to put in a rep's hands, or: none}
Result: {the overall verdict, exactly as stated at the top of this report}
```

## Honesty rules

- Judge the card, not the rivalry. Whether the competitor deserves to lose is outside the review; whether this card tells its own side the truth is the whole of it.
- Do not supply competitive facts from outside knowledge to fill the card's gaps, and do not mark a claim sourced because the reviewer happens to know it is true. The criterion judges the card's own evidence trail, because the rep can only repeat what the card can defend.
- Where a claim's truth cannot be assessed from the material provided, judge its sourcing and currency, and say the review took the claim as stated. The report must never read as independent verification of claims about a competitor.
- Treat disparagement as a defect, not a style. Language on the card that a rep could not repeat to the competitor's face without embarrassment is a finding under honesty, because buyers hear tone as clearly as content.
- Landmines are judged as questions asked in good faith about real needs. A "question" whose only function is to plant an unverified claim about the competitor is not a landmine; it is an unsourced claim wearing a question mark, and it is judged under criterion 1.

## Sources

The card anatomy and disciplines in this rubric follow Klue's published battlecard guidance (Klue, "8 Essential Sales Battlecards You Need to Know" and related competitive enablement material, klue.com): the component set (competitor overview, questions to ask, landmines to lay, objection handling, pricing, why we win and why we lose), the insistence that content stay short, digestible, and linked to proof, and the principle that the strongest battlecard content comes from win and loss evidence, the buyer's own account of why deals were won and lost, rather than internal assumptions about the competitor. The say-and-show formulation in criterion 4 reflects the same guidance's finding that cards combining talk tracks with proof assets are the ones sellers retain and use.
