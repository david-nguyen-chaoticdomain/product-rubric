## Purpose

Use this rubric to review a research readout before it circulates. A readout is where research becomes organizational fact: its sentences get quoted in roadmaps, planning documents, and decisions, usually stripped of their original caution within a week. Flaws that were merely untidy in a researcher's notes become load-bearing errors at this stage, a preference three participants mentioned hardening into "users want," an interpretation settling into the record as something users said.

This review audits the readout as a chain of claims. For each significant claim it asks: what evidence does the readout itself present for this, is the claim the same size as that evidence, and would a careful reader be able to tell the difference between what participants did and what the researcher concluded? It also judges the readout's completeness as evidence: whether the contradicting participant made it into the report, and whether the basis (how many people, who they were, what kind of study) is stated where a reader can weigh it.

The review does not re-run the study and does not judge whether the research was well conducted; that is the Study Plan Review's territory, before the fact. It judges the document in front of it. A well-conducted study can produce a readout that fails this review, and a weak study can produce a readout that passes it, by claiming only what its evidence carries. If the input is raw evidence rather than a readout (notes, transcripts, a data export), say so and stop; there is nothing yet to audit.

## Criteria

Judge each criterion Holds, Needs work, or Missing, using the Scoring section. Every judgment cites the readout's own sentences.

### 1. Claims trace to evidence
Every significant claim in the readout rests on evidence the reader can inspect.
- What to examine: The readout's claims one by one against what it presents under them: quotes, counts, observed behaviors, named sessions or responses. Claims resting on phrases like "users felt" or "participants struggled" with nothing concrete attached; numbers that appear without their base ("most" of how many); charts whose underlying counts the reader cannot recover.
- What good looks like: A skeptical reader can, for any claim that matters, find the evidence for it inside the document and judge it for themselves.
- What bad looks like: A headline finding whose entire support is the sentence that states it; evidence presented for the claims the author found interesting and omitted for the rest.

### 2. Observation and interpretation stay separate
What participants did and said is not silently upgraded into why, or into what it means.
- What to examine: The language of findings for interpretation wearing observation's clothing ("users were confused by the pricing" where the evidence shows hesitation, not confusion); motives assigned without the participant saying them; the researcher's explanations interleaved with the record so a reader cannot tell where one ends and the other begins. Interpretation is legitimate in a readout; this criterion judges whether it is labeled as interpretation when it appears.
- What good looks like: The reader always knows which sentences report the study and which sentences are the researcher thinking, and the thinking is presented as thinking.
- What bad looks like: A findings section where conclusions and observations alternate unlabeled, so the quotable sentence a stakeholder lifts is an inference traveling as a fact.

### 3. Disconfirming evidence is present
The readout includes the evidence that argues against its own headlines.
- What to examine: Participants who behaved or answered against the main findings; segments for whom a finding reversed; findings stated universally that the underlying material only supports for some. Whether exceptions are quantified or at least named, or whether the readout reads as if the study found one clean story.
- What good looks like: Every headline finding carries its boundary: who it did not hold for, and how much of the evidence ran the other way.
- What bad looks like: A finding from five of six participants written as the finding, with the sixth nowhere in the document; a segment the product serves badly, absent from the readout because it complicates the summary.

### 4. Recommendations stay proportional
What the readout asks the team to do is sized to what the study can support.
- What to examine: Each recommendation against the strength and breadth of its evidence: exploratory interviews producing a company-wide mandate; a preference signal producing a rebuild commitment; recommendations with no finding under them at all. Whether the readout distinguishes what the evidence compels, what it suggests, and what it merely raises as a question for further study.
- What good looks like: A reader can feel the difference in confidence the readout itself feels, because recommendations are graded in the author's own language.
- What bad looks like: Every recommendation written at the same certainty; the largest ask in the document resting on the smallest evidence in it.

### 5. The basis is stated honestly
A reader can weigh the readout because its foundations are printed on it.
- What to examine: Whether the readout states how many participants or responses it rests on, who they were (and how they were recruited, when that shapes the findings), what kind of study produced it, and when. Whether simulated, proxy, or secondary evidence is labeled as what it is wherever it appears. Whether limits the author knows about (a skewed sample, a prototype that behaved unlike the product) appear in the document rather than only in the author's head.
- What good looks like: Someone who reads only the readout, months later, in another document's footnote, can still reconstruct how much weight its claims can bear.
- What bad looks like: Percentages with no visible denominator anywhere; a readout indistinguishable in tone between a 400-response survey and a four-person interview study.

### 6. Participant voice is representative
The human material in the readout shows the range of what was heard, not only its most quotable edge.
- What to examine: Quotes for whether they carry the findings or merely decorate them; whether the same participant's voice recurs because it was vivid; whether quotes are trimmed in ways that change their meaning; whether quiet, typical responses appear alongside the extreme ones the room will remember. Also whether participants are represented with basic fairness: not mocked, not flattened into caricatures that serve the narrative.
- What good looks like: A reader meeting only this document forms a picture of the participants that the raw material would recognize.
- What bad looks like: Three quotes from one articulate participant carrying a study of twelve; a quote cut mid-sentence at exactly the point where it stopped agreeing with the finding.

## Scoring

This rubric gives no numeric score. Each criterion receives one verdict:

- **Holds**: the readout meets this criterion; a reader can rely on it in this respect.
- **Needs work**: the criterion is attempted but a specific passage or pattern undermines it. The finding names the passage and what would repair it.
- **Missing**: the readout makes no attempt at this criterion (no basis stated anywhere, no disconfirming evidence in the document), or the attempt is so thin it cannot be relied on.

In addition, the review performs a claim audit. Extract the readout's significant claims (the findings and assertions a stakeholder would quote; up to eight, choosing the most consequential) and rate each one:

- **Supported**: the readout presents evidence under it that matches the claim's size.
- **Partly supported**: evidence exists but the claim is larger, broader, or more certain than it.
- **Unsupported**: the readout presents no inspectable evidence for it, or the evidence it presents argues the other way.

Claims are rated against the evidence in the readout itself. When the underlying study material was also supplied, claims may additionally be checked against it, and any claim the readout's evidence supports but the underlying material contradicts is rated Unsupported with the contradiction named. If the underlying material was not supplied, the report says so once, plainly, so readers know the audit's limit.

Overall reliance verdict, stated once at the top, in exactly one of these forms:

- **Can be relied on**: no criterion below Holds except at most one Needs work, and no claim rated Unsupported.
- **Rely on it with the noted gaps**: any other combination short of the line below; the gaps travel with the readout until fixed.
- **Do not circulate yet**: any criterion Missing on Claims trace to evidence or The basis is stated honestly, or two or more claims rated Unsupported. In this state the readout would mislead even careful readers, and fixing it is the author's next task, not the audience's.

## Output format

Return the review in this structure, in Markdown:

1. **Reliance verdict: [Can be relied on / Rely on it with the noted gaps / Do not circulate yet]**, with one sentence of reason, and a line stating whether the underlying study material was available to the review.
2. **Claim audit**: a table or list of the audited claims, each quoted or tightly paraphrased with its rating and the evidence named, or its absence noted.
3. One section per criterion, in the order above, with its verdict and findings tied to specific passages of the readout.
4. A closing section, **Fixes before this circulates**, in priority order: unsupported and oversized claims first, then the missing basis, then the rest.

Example of a finding at the standard this rubric expects:
- Acceptable: "Partly supported: the readout claims 'users complete checkout without help.' The evidence shown is four of six participants completing it, one needing two hints (mentioned in the appendix), and one abandoning, who does not appear in the findings section. The claim the evidence supports is 'most participants in this study completed checkout, with the payment step causing the failures.'"
- Not acceptable: "The findings feel overstated in places." (It names no claim, no evidence, and no repair.)

Finally, end the report with the tracking summary block, in exactly this form. Every value in the block is copied or counted from the judgments in this report; the block adds no new judgment of its own. It is the record a team keeps when it runs this rubric repeatedly and wants to see whether its work is improving.

```
Rubric summary, for tracking
Rubric: Findings Review v1.0 · Date: {the date of this run} · Artifact: {the artifact's name as given in the request, or: not named}
Criteria: Holds {n} · Needs work {n} · Missing {n} · Claims audited {n}: Supported {n} · Partly supported {n} · Unsupported {n}
Gate criteria not met: {the criteria whose grade held the verdict below Can be relied on, or: none}
Result: {the reliance verdict, exactly as stated at the top of this report}
```

## Honesty rules

- Audit the document, not the researcher. Every finding is about a passage and its support, never about the author's rigor, motives, or skill.
- Do not import outside knowledge as evidence. A claim the reviewer happens to believe from other studies is still Unsupported here if this readout presents nothing under it; the audit judges support, not truth.
- Rate claim size precisely: a claim can be true of the study and still oversized for it ("users" for "five of our six participants, all existing customers"). The correction offered for an oversized claim is the claim at the size its evidence supports, written out in the finding.
- Resist both directions of inflation: do not soften an Unsupported rating because the readout is otherwise good, and do not harden ratings to make the review feel rigorous. A readout that passes this review cleanly is a real and reportable result.
- Where a passage could be read two ways, quote it and judge the reading a busy stakeholder would take away, because that is the reading the readout will live by once circulated.
