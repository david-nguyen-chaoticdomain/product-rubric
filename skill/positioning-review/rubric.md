## Purpose

Use this rubric to review a positioning document before messaging, sales material, and product decisions are built on it. Positioning fails quietly. Each of its components can look plausible on its own, a competitor list, a differentiator, a value claim, a customer profile, a category, while the chain between them is broken: attributes that are not unique to anyone, value that does not follow from the attributes claimed, a customer chosen because they are large rather than because they care. Nothing downstream reports the break. It surfaces months later as messaging that could belong to any product in the market and a sales team improvising the real positioning call by call.

This review judges the document against the five components of effective positioning from April Dunford's Obviously Awesome: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value (and proof), target market characteristics, and market category, plus a sixth judgment of this rubric's own, whether the five cohere into one chain. It judges the positioning as documented. It cannot judge whether the claims are commercially true against evidence it was not given, and it says so where that matters.

The review does not write positioning and does not choose the category. It also does not judge messaging: the words a homepage or a deck uses to express positioning are the Messaging Review's territory. If the input is finished messaging rather than positioning (homepage copy, an ad, a pitch), say so and stop; positioning may be implied by it, but there is no positioning document here to audit.

## Criteria

Judge each criterion Holds, Needs work, or Missing, using the Scoring section. Every judgment cites the document's own text.

### 1. Competitive alternatives are the real ones
The alternatives named are what customers would actually do if this product did not exist, including the unglamorous ones.
- What to examine: Whether the list includes the status quo (the spreadsheet, the manual process, the general-purpose tool, doing nothing) alongside named rival products, since in most markets the status quo is the alternative that wins. Whether the alternatives are drawn from the customer's world (what buyers shortlist and what deals are lost to) or from the vendor's (the two rivals whose names the team knows). Whether the document can say where its list came from: lost deals, customer interviews, review sites.
- What good looks like: A reader believes this list because it contains at least one alternative the vendor gains nothing by naming, and because every later claim in the document is measured against this list and no other.
- What bad looks like: Two famous direct competitors and silence about the workaround most customers use today; a list that happens to contain exactly the products this one already beats.

### 2. Unique attributes are actually unique
Each claimed differentiator is a capability or fact the named alternatives lack, not a benefit, a hope, or a feature everyone has.
- What to examine: Attribute by attribute: could a named alternative truthfully claim this too? Whether attributes are stated as capabilities (what the product has or does) rather than as value in disguise ("easy to use" is a claim about value, not an attribute). Whether attributes the whole category shares (secure, reliable, integrates with) are presented as differentiation. Whether any attribute is unique only because the alternatives were defined narrowly in the criterion above.
- What good looks like: Every attribute survives the test "say this sentence about the leading alternative and it becomes false," and the list is short enough to be the actual reasons customers choose this product.
- What bad looks like: A differentiator list that reads as the category's table stakes; uniqueness asserted by adjective ("industry-leading," "best-in-class") where a checkable capability was needed.

### 3. Value follows from the attributes, and proof exists
Each value claim traces to a unique attribute above it, and the proof a skeptic would ask for is present or honestly absent.
- What to examine: The chain, claim by claim: which attribute produces this benefit, and for whom. Value claims with no attribute under them (orphan promises the product's uniqueness cannot explain). Proof in the document's own material: customer results, named testimonials, measured outcomes, credible third parties, or an explicit admission that proof is still being gathered. Whether value is stated in the customer's terms (an outcome they budget for) or in the vendor's (engagement, synergy, empowerment).
- What good looks like: A reader can draw the line from any value claim down to the attribute that causes it, and can see what evidence exists that the value is real, stated at its actual strength.
- What bad looks like: A value section that could be pasted onto a competitor unchanged; proof by logo wall, where the customers shown are not claimed to have achieved the value described.

### 4. Target market characteristics are specific
The best-fit customer is described by the traits that make them care about this value more than others do, not by size alone.
- What to examine: Whether the characteristics are the kind that predict caring (a situation, a maturity, a pain threshold, a triggering event) rather than only firmographics (industry, headcount, region) that any rival could target identically. Whether the document describes who buys quickly and happily, the customers the value fits best, or a total addressable market wearing a target's clothes. Whether it is honest about who is a poor fit, which is the same knowledge viewed from the other side.
- What good looks like: A sales leader could exclude real prospects using this description, and would want to, because the traits named are the ones that make the value in criterion 3 land.
- What bad looks like: "Mid-market companies that value efficiency"; a target that is everyone the sales team has ever sold to, widened until the positioning fits them all, which means it leads none of them anywhere.

### 5. Market category is chosen, not defaulted
The category is a deliberate frame whose assumptions help the product, selected with its costs visible.
- What to examine: Whether the document names the category it claims and shows that the choice was made (the alternatives considered, why this frame makes the value obvious). What the category makes customers assume: the competitors they will compare against, the features they will consider mandatory, the price range they will expect, and whether the product survives those assumptions. Whether an invented or reframed category is argued for with its adoption cost acknowledged, or asserted as free.
- What good looks like: The category is the one in which this product's unique attributes look like the criteria that matter, and the document shows the comparison customers will run and is content to be run through it.
- What bad looks like: The category is whatever the leading rival calls itself, inherited without a sentence of reasoning; or a coined category with no account of what it costs to teach the market a new word.

### 6. The five components cohere
Read as one argument, the components support each other instead of merely coexisting.
- What to examine: Whether the value (3) is valued by the customer described in (4) for the reasons the attributes (2) provide against the alternatives named in (1), inside the frame set by (5). Contradictions between components: a best-fit customer who buys on the dimension the product is weakest on; a category whose mandatory features the attribute list does not cover; trends invoked (the framework's optional sixth component) that are real and current for these buyers rather than decoration. Whether any component appears to have been written to flatter a different product than the other four describe.
- What good looks like: Changing any one component would force changes in the others, because each is load-bearing in the same structure.
- What bad looks like: Five individually polished sections that describe, on inspection, two different products and three different customers.

## Scoring

This rubric gives no numeric score. Each criterion receives one verdict:

- **Holds**: the document meets this criterion; messaging and sales material can be built on it in this respect.
- **Needs work**: the criterion is attempted but a specific gap undermines it. The finding names the gap and what would repair it.
- **Missing**: the document makes no attempt at this criterion (no alternatives named at all, no proof of any kind discussed), or the attempt is so thin it cannot carry weight.

Overall verdict, stated once at the top, in exactly one of these forms:

- **Holds together**: no criterion below Holds except at most one Needs work.
- **Holds with the noted gaps**: any other combination short of the line below; the gaps are the positioning work the team still owes, and downstream material inherits them until they are repaired.
- **Does not hold together yet**: any criterion Missing on Competitive alternatives are the real ones or Unique attributes are actually unique, or Cohere rated Missing. In this state the document is a set of marketing sentences, not a position, and building messaging on it produces copy that any rival could sign.

## Output format

Return the review in this structure, in Markdown:

1. **Verdict: [Holds together / Holds with the noted gaps / Does not hold together yet]**, with one sentence of reason, and a line stating what customer evidence, if any, was available to the review beyond the document.
2. One section per criterion, in the order above, with its verdict and findings tied to specific components and passages of the document.
3. A closing section, **Positioning work still owed**, in priority order: alternatives and attributes first, then the value chain and its proof, then the customer and category decisions.

Example of a finding at the standard this rubric expects:
- Acceptable: "Needs work: the attributes list claims 'real-time collaboration,' but the leading alternative named in the same document ships real-time collaboration, and its help pages describe it. The attribute the evidence in this document actually supports is narrower and stronger: 'the only alternative that keeps an audit trail of every change,' which no named alternative is shown to have."
- Not acceptable: "The positioning could be more differentiated." (It names no component, no claim, and no repair.)

Finally, end the report with the tracking summary block, in exactly this form. Every value in the block is copied or counted from the judgments in this report; the block adds no new judgment of its own. It is the record a team keeps when it runs this rubric repeatedly and wants to see whether its work is improving.

```
Rubric summary, for tracking
Rubric: Positioning Review v1.0 · Date: {the date of this run} · Artifact: {the artifact's name as given in the request, or: not named}
Criteria: Holds {n} · Needs work {n} · Missing {n}
Gate criteria not met: {the criteria whose grade held the verdict below Holds together, or: none}
Result: {the overall verdict, exactly as stated at the top of this report}
```

## Honesty rules

- Judge the document, not the market and not the team. Whether this product deserves to win its category is outside the review; whether the document's claims cohere and carry proof is the whole of it.
- Do not verify claims from outside knowledge and then credit the document with the verification. Where the reviewer happens to know an attribute is genuinely unique in the market, the criterion still judges whether the document establishes it; a true claim the document cannot support is Needs work with the missing support named.
- Do not reward a component for being well written. A beautifully phrased value claim with no attribute under it and no proof beside it fails the same way a clumsy one does.
- Keep the framework's discipline: attributes are capabilities, value is what they cause, and the two must not be graded as one merged impression. Most positioning failures hide in exactly that merge.
- Where the reviewer cannot tell what customers actually do instead, say so. The alternatives criterion judges the list as argued in the document; it does not certify the list against the market, and the report must not read as if it did.
