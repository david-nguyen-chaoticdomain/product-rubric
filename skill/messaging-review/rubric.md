## Purpose

Use this rubric to review a messaging document before it becomes the words an organization repeats. Messaging is positioning translated into language a buyer hears, and it decays in predictable ways: the insight that made the value matter gets dropped because the feature list was easier to write, proof quietly detaches from the claims it was meant to carry, and the customer, who was the subject of the strategy, becomes the audience for a monologue about the vendor. Nobody notices the decay in the document. They notice it in the field, as deals lost to no decision and prospects who cannot repeat back what the product is for.

This review judges the message as a story told to a buyer, following the structure April Dunford sets out in Sales Pitch: lead with an insight, engage the alternatives honestly, define what an ideal solution must do, let the differentiated value carry the weight, prove it, answer the objections, and close with a clear ask. It adds one discipline from Donald Miller's Building a StoryBrand: the customer is the hero of the message and the brand is the guide, never the reverse.

The review judges the messaging document. A live homepage, an ad, or a finished deck is an expression of messaging and may also fall under the Copy Review; this rubric's subject is the source document those expressions are built from. If the input is only finished copy with no messaging structure behind it, say so and stop. The review also does not judge the positioning underneath the message; that is the Positioning Review's work, and a message faithfully expressing weak positioning is reported as exactly that, not repaired here.

## Criteria

Judge each criterion Holds, Needs work, or Missing, using the Scoring section. Every judgment cites the document's own language.

### 1. Leads with an insight, not the product
The message opens with a specific point of view about the customer's problem that makes the value that follows matter.
- What to examine: Whether there is an insight at all, a claim about the customer's world that a thoughtful buyer would find clarifying ("the problem inside the problem"), or whether the document opens with the vendor, the product, or a trend so broad it could open anyone's deck ("AI is transforming business"). Whether the insight, if the buyer accepts it, naturally raises the importance of exactly the value this product uniquely offers, which is how Dunford's structure earns its opening.
- What good looks like: A buyer who has never heard of the product finishes the first section understanding their own situation differently, and wanting the criteria that follow.
- What bad looks like: An insight section that is a market-size statistic; a document whose first product-free paragraph could be deleted without changing anything the rest of it says.

### 2. Engages the alternatives honestly
The message names the real ways customers solve this problem today, states their trade-offs fairly, and sets the criteria an ideal answer must meet.
- What to examine: Whether the alternatives in the message match the ones the positioning names, including the status quo; whether rival approaches are described in terms their own users would accept before their limits are drawn, since a buyer who uses one of those alternatives and hears it caricatured stops listening. Whether the message articulates the perfect-world test, the criteria any good solution must satisfy, in a form the buyer can apply to everyone, including this vendor.
- What good looks like: A buyer currently using a competing approach feels understood first and persuaded second, and leaves with a checklist that happens to describe the shape of this product.
- What bad looks like: Strawman competitors who exist only to lose; a message that behaves as if the buyer has no current way of doing this at all, when nearly every buyer does.

### 3. Differentiated value carries the hierarchy
The lead message is the one thing this product does that alternatives cannot, and everything beneath it is arranged as support.
- What to examine: Whether the core message and the first pillar are the differentiated value, or whether the hierarchy is ordered by the internal org chart (the newest feature, the loudest team's roadmap). The swap test, applied to the headline claims: could the leading alternative run this sentence unchanged? Whether features appear as evidence under value claims rather than as claims themselves, and whether minor capabilities are given equal billing with the differentiator until the message has no center.
- What good looks like: A reader skimming only the headings can repeat the differentiated value, and the pillars read as three reasons it is true rather than three products sharing a document.
- What bad looks like: A hierarchy of five equal pillars, two of which every competitor also claims; the differentiator appearing in paragraph four, beneath the integrations list.

### 4. Proof is attached, at the right strength
Every material claim carries the strongest proof available, and the proof shown matches the size of the claim it carries.
- What to examine: Claim by claim: case studies with named customers and measured results; third-party validation; testimonials; statistics; awards, in roughly that order of credibility, which is the order Dunford's structure implies. Proof that floats in a separate section, attached to no claim. Superlatives ("leading," "only," "first") with nothing under them. Numbers whose source and date the document does not give, in a message that will be repeated for a year.
- What good looks like: The largest claim in the document has the strongest proof in the document directly beneath it, and a sales rep asked "says who?" can answer from the page.
- What bad looks like: A logo wall offered as proof of outcomes the logos never commented on; a single friendly quote carrying the document's central value claim.

### 5. The customer is the subject
The message's hero is the customer and their problem; the brand appears as a guide who understands it and can help, in that order.
- What to examine: The grammar of the document: how many sentences are about the customer's situation, work, and stakes versus about the vendor's platform, vision, and journey. Whether the guide's two credentials are both present: empathy (language showing the problem is understood from the inside) and authority (evidence the vendor can actually help, which ties back to criterion 4). Whether the customer's desired outcome is described as a change in their world or as adoption of the product.
- What good looks like: A prospect reading the message feels described before they feel sold to, and the vendor enters the story at the moment the customer needs help, carrying proof.
- What bad looks like: A narrative whose protagonist is the company's founding insight; success defined as becoming a customer; empathy performed in one stock sentence ("we know compliance is hard") before forty slides about features.

### 6. Objections are anticipated, and the ask is clear
The document arms its reader for the doubts a real buyer raises, and it ends by naming the next step.
- What to examine: Whether the known objections (price, switching cost, security, the incumbent's counter-claim, the internal champion's political risk) appear with honest answers that concede what is true, or whether the document pretends the sale has no friction. Whether the ask is a specific, low-ambiguity next step per audience (a pilot with a shape, a review with a date) or a general invitation to "learn more." Whether different audiences, an economic buyer, a user, a technical reviewer, each find their objection answered in the message meant for them.
- What good looks like: A champion inside the customer's company could defend this purchase in a meeting the vendor will never attend, using only this document.
- What bad looks like: An objections section answering only the easy questions sales enjoys answering; a closing ask so soft that a persuaded buyer would not know what to do next.

## Scoring

This rubric gives no numeric score. Each criterion receives one verdict:

- **Holds**: the document meets this criterion; the message can be repeated in this respect.
- **Needs work**: the criterion is attempted but a specific gap undermines it. The finding names the gap and what would repair it.
- **Missing**: the document makes no attempt at this criterion (no insight anywhere, no proof attached to any claim), or the attempt is so thin it cannot carry weight.

Overall verdict, stated once at the top, in exactly one of these forms:

- **Fit to publish and sell from**: no criterion below Holds except at most one Needs work.
- **Usable with the noted gaps**: any other combination short of the line below; the gaps are inherited by every page, deck, and call built on this document until they are repaired.
- **Do not publish yet**: any criterion Missing on Leads with an insight, not the product, Differentiated value carries the hierarchy, or Proof is attached, at the right strength. In this state the document is a feature announcement with ambitions, and publishing it teaches the market a message the team will spend quarters un-teaching.

## Output format

Return the review in this structure, in Markdown:

1. **Verdict: [Fit to publish and sell from / Usable with the noted gaps / Do not publish yet]**, with one sentence of reason, and a line stating whether the positioning behind this message was available to the review.
2. One section per criterion, in the order above, with its verdict and findings tied to specific messages, pillars, and proof points in the document.
3. A closing section, **Fixes before this is repeated**, in priority order: the insight and the differentiated value first, then proof, then objections and the ask.

Example of a finding at the standard this rubric expects:
- Acceptable: "Needs work: the lead pillar claims the product 'removes the busywork from compliance.' The proof beneath it is one testimonial praising the support team. Either the claim shrinks to what the proof carries, or the document needs the measured result it implies, such as the hours a named customer stopped spending on audit preparation, with the customer and date attached."
- Not acceptable: "The messaging needs more social proof." (It names no claim, no proof gap, and no repair.)

Finally, end the report with the summary block, in exactly this form. Every value in the block is copied or counted from the judgments in this report; the block adds no new judgment of its own. It is the record a team keeps when it runs this rubric repeatedly and wants to see whether its work is improving.

```
Rubric summary
Rubric: Messaging Review v1.0 · Date: {the date of this run} · Artifact: {the artifact's name as given in the request, or: not named}
Criteria: Holds {n} · Needs work {n} · Missing {n}
Gate criteria not met: {the criteria whose grade held the verdict below Fit to publish and sell from, or: none}
Result: {the overall verdict, exactly as stated at the top of this report}
```

## Honesty rules

- Judge the message, not the product and not the positioning. Where the message faithfully expresses positioning that looks weak, say that the weakness sits upstream and name the Positioning Review as its venue; do not quietly rewrite the strategy inside a messaging finding.
- Do not certify claims as true. The review judges whether proof is attached and whether it is sized to the claim, using the material provided; it does not establish that a customer's results were real, and its language must not drift into vouching for them.
- Apply the swap test without charity in either direction: a headline a rival could run is a finding even when the team loves the sentence, and a genuinely distinctive claim is credited even when its phrasing is clumsy. Craft notes belong to the Copy Review; this rubric judges structure and support.
- Do not reward objection answers for confidence. An answer that concedes a real limitation and reframes honestly outranks a deflection, and the review says so when it finds the deflection.
- Where the document addresses several audiences, judge each audience's message as that audience will hear it in isolation, because that is how each of them will in fact hear it.

## Sources

This rubric is built primarily on the eight-step sales pitch structure from April Dunford, "Sales Pitch" (2023): insight, alternatives, perfect world, introduction, differentiated value, proof, objections, and the ask, adapted here from judging a live pitch to judging the messaging document a pitch is built from.
