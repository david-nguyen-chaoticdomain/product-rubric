# Worked example: Five Second Test

**Fixture:** `fixtures/nexus-homepage/homepage-b-above-fold.png` (the Nexus marketing homepage, Option B dark direction, above the fold; the fixture brief is in the same folder).
**Rubric:** Five Second Test (`five-second-test`), version 1.0, fetched through the ProductRubric MCP server.
**Produced:** Oct 20, 2026, by an AI assistant running the rubric exactly as written. The panel is simulated, as the rubric's Procedure specifies: five first-time visitors, each shown the whole screen for five seconds, then asked what they remember. No real users took part, and the counts below are the simulation's reasoned estimates, not measurements.

---

**Memorability score: 7.3 / 10**

Five seconds is enough for this page's main claim and its main action to land: a first-time visitor leaves knowing this is a project platform for teams and that the next step is to start free. What the five seconds lose is the page's only news, the Sprints 2.0 announcement, which is set as the smallest text in the hero and survives for almost no one.

## Remembered

- **The headline, "The project platform engineered for high-growth teams": 5 of 5.** It is the largest type on the screen, centered, white on near-black, and it sits where scanning starts. Every simulated participant repeats its gist back, and most keep the phrase "high-growth teams."
- **The "Get Started Free" button: 4 of 5.** Saturated indigo against the dark page, directly under the subhead, and repeated in the navigation at the top right, so the eye meets it twice. The participant who spends the five seconds inside the product visual does not register which button was primary.
- **The Nexus brand (logo and name, top left): 4 of 5.** Top left is the habitual first fixation, and the name repeats in the announcement pill and in the product visual's chrome ("Nexus / Workspace"), so even a participant who misses the wordmark absorbs the name. One participant remembers "a purple logo" without the name attached.
- **The product visual (a monitor showing a dark dashboard): 4 of 5.** It is the biggest object on the page and the only image, so it is remembered as an object. What it shows does not survive: no participant can say what the dashboard's rings or columns meant, because at a glance they are texture, small and dark inside a dark frame.
- **The subhead's promise, "Nexus unites tasks, docs, sprints, and roadmaps in a single workspace": 3 of 5.** The four concrete nouns carry the product definition and three participants repeat the gist ("tasks and docs and roadmaps in one place"). The line is small and muted under a very large headline, and the other two participants keep only the headline's broader claim.

## Likely missed

- **The announcement pill, "Introducing Nexus Sprints 2.0": 2 of 5.** It sits directly above the headline, where eyes pass on the way to the big type, and it is set in the smallest text in the hero. Two participants catch "something 2.0" because the numeral is a distinctive token; the rest never register the launch at all.

## Highest impact fixes

1. Decide what the announcement pill is for. It is the page's only news and its least memorable element. If the Sprints 2.0 launch matters this quarter, give the pill the size and contrast of an element meant to be read, or carry the launch in the headline's own hierarchy. If it is a whisper for returning visitors, its current weight is honest and this fix is unnecessary.
2. Protect the subhead's gist. The four-noun promise is the product definition, and only 3 of 5 carry it away. Setting it one step larger, or trimming it to the three nouns the rest of the page actually shows (tasks, docs, roadmaps), would raise recall of the sentence the whole pitch depends on.
3. Make the product visual show one legible thing. Its size already wins attention; a zoomed slice with one readable board or metric would convert that attention into a remembered capability instead of a remembered monitor.
